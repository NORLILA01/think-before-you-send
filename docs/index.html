Practise the conversation before it happens

Group assignments, peer feedback and online discussions can involve frustration or disagreement. A useful task is to express the concern without attacking the person. Feeling angry or disagreeing is not, by itself, aggression.

Pause, reflect, rewrite and recheck. AI can suggest a next step; the student chooses the words. An educator can guide discussion of the student's reasoning.

  1. Pause

    Write your draft before sending it anywhere else.

  2. Reflect

    Read the wording feedback. For a flagged draft, optional AI coaching offers a possible feeling, how it may land, and a tip. These suggestions may be wrong.

  3. Rewrite

    Describe the situation, name your concern and make a clear request, in your own words.

  4. Recheck

    Check again and decide. Passing the checker does not guarantee a message is appropriate.

What students practise

  • Emotional awareness: put a possible feeling or need into words.
  • Perspective-taking: consider how another person might read a message.
  • Self-regulation: create a pause before responding impulsively.
  • Constructive communication: replace personal attacks with specific concerns and requests.
  • Critical AI literacy: question automated feedback and explain your choices.

These are intended learning aims. Improvement in these skills has not yet been measured in a student study.

An example to discuss

A teammate has missed an agreed deadline. This is a prepared teaching example, not a recorded participant outcome or a guaranteed model response.

Initial draft

“You are such an idiot. You never do your part.”

It attacks the person and gives no next step.

Possible student revision

“I'm frustratedA that the slides aren't readyB because our deadline is tomorrow. Can you send your section by 6 pm, or tell us what help you need?C”

  1. Feeling: keeps the frustration visible.
  2. Situation: names what happened instead of labelling the person.
  3. Request: gives a concrete next step.

A short activity for a tutorial

Use a fictional disagreement that fits your class. Ask students to write a first response, review the feedback and revise, then explain what they changed and why.

Educators assess the reasoning. The detector's label should not be used as a grade or as a judgement about a student's character. Students can take part using local tips alone, without requesting AI coaching.

Discussion prompts

  • What concern did you want to communicate?
  • Which phrase might make the recipient defensive?
  • Did you agree with the tool's feedback? Why or why not?
  • How does your revision keep your point while respecting the other person?

Proposed 10-minute activity

  1. 2 minDraft
  2. 3 minReview and revise
  3. 3 minDiscuss
  4. 2 minReflect

These are suggested teaching timings, not the parameters of a tested intervention.

What has been tested, and what has not

Technical results are a starting point. They describe how a classifier behaves on one public dataset, not whether students learn.

Precision at the recorded threshold0.60
Recall at the recorded threshold0.53
DataCivil Comments public-comment test split (CC0)
LabelRater toxicity, insult or threat score of at least 0.5 (a proxy)

Roughly 60% of flagged examples matched the project's positive label, and roughly 53% of positive examples were flagged. Source: the detector README in the project repository, not reproduced by this website.

Technical detail

Word unigrams and bigrams (40,000 terms) with TF-IDF weighting and logistic regression. ROC-AUC 0.903 and PR-AUC 0.604 on the same held-out set; decision threshold 0.68. ROC-AUC is a ranking measure and is not an accuracy percentage.

Student learning outcomes: not yet evaluated

No study has shown that this prototype improves emotional intelligence or reduces aggressive behaviour. Behaviour change and fairness across groups have also not been measured.

Current limits

  • Built for English. Malay and mixed-language use have not been validated.
  • It can flag harmless wording and miss indirect hostility.
  • Training data came from public comments, not university conversations.
  • A fairness audit has not been carried out.
  • No clinical validation. It is a communication aid, not an assessment of anyone's intentions, emotions or risk.
  • Not flagged does not mean safe or respectful in every context.

About the project and your privacy

The project began by exploring open-source work on emotional intelligence, emotion regulation and aggression detection. It now combines a browser-based wording check with optional communication coaching. The student stays responsible for writing the response.

How information moves

The first check runs in your browser. If you request AI coaching, the checked draft is sent through a Cloudflare Worker to OpenRouter and the configured model provider. Their processing policies apply.

  1. GitHub Pages sends the website and detector files to the browser.
  2. The draft is checked locally in the browser.
  3. The browser shows feedback and, for flagged drafts, an optional coaching action.
  4. Only a coaching request sends that checked draft to the Cloudflare Worker.
  5. The Worker calls OpenRouter and the configured language-model provider.
  6. Suggestions return to the browser, and the student decides how to revise.

The application code has no message-history database and does not log message text. That does not establish how hosting or AI providers retain data. Use fictional or anonymised examples and avoid confidential information.

This is an educational communication aid, not a clinical assessment or treatment service. If you may hurt yourself, contact local emergency services or a crisis line; findahelpline.com lists services by country.
